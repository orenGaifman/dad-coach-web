# Color System

## Why Color Matters

Color is the first thing a father registers — before reading a single word. The palette must communicate safety, calm, and warmth instantly. It must never feel clinical (whites and blues of healthcare), childish (primary colors), or aggressive (high contrast and saturation).

## Emotional Intent

### Should Create

- Calm and groundedness
- Warmth without playfulness
- Trust and reliability
- Focus without tension
- Subtle energy for progress moments

### Should Avoid

- Medical sterility (cold whites, clinical blues)
- Corporate flatness (generic grays, business blue)
- Childishness (bright primaries, rainbow palettes)
- Aggression (high saturation, stark contrasts)
- Gloom (desaturation to the point of lifelessness)

## Palette Strategy

### Foundation: Warm Neutrals

The base palette is built on warm neutrals — off-whites, soft stones, and deep warm grays. These create a canvas that feels like natural materials (paper, wood, linen) rather than digital screens.

- Background tones should lean warm (cream, sand) rather than cool (blue-gray)
- Text should be near-black with warm undertone, never pure #000000
- Surface hierarchy communicated through subtle warmth shifts, not stark contrast

### Primary: Earthy Calm

The primary accent color should feel natural and grounded — think deep sage, warm terracotta, or muted navy. It's used sparingly for primary actions and key navigation.

Qualities:
- Desaturated enough to feel mature
- Warm enough to feel human
- Distinct enough to guide attention
- Accessible against both light and dark surfaces

### Progress & Achievement: Earned Warmth

The belt system and achievement moments use color that feels *earned* — warm golds, deep greens, rich ambers. These colors appear only when a father has progressed, making them meaningful rather than decorative.

- Colors intensify as belts advance
- Never neon or electric
- Should feel like natural materials (leather, brass, aged wood)

### Semantic: Clear but Gentle

Error, warning, and success states use recognizable semantic colors but softened:

- Errors are warm reds, not alarming fire-engine red
- Success is natural green, not synthetic neon
- Warnings are amber, not cautionary yellow
- Information is soft blue, not corporate

### Dark Mode Considerations

Dark mode inverts warmth, not removes it. Dark backgrounds should feel like evening — warm charcoals, not cold black. The emotional quality remains unchanged regardless of mode.

## Principles

1. **Never more than two colors competing for attention** on any single view
2. **Color density decreases as content importance increases** — coaching conversations are nearly monochrome
3. **Achievement color is rare** — its scarcity is what makes it meaningful
4. **Backgrounds are warm and quiet** — they support content, never compete
5. **Accessibility is non-negotiable** — all combinations meet WCAG AA minimum, critical text meets AAA
